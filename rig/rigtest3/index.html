MOUSE STAND-IN — this is not head tracking

rigtest3 setup · one ZED on the base

Four steps, a couple of minutes. Everything is saved in this browser as you go. Press S at any time to come back here, Esc to go to the rig view. There is no pair to calibrate: the ZED's two eyes are one factory-measured camera. What this asks for instead is where the camera stands (step 2) — and even the angle it is propped at can be measured from your own head with A in the rig view.

Stand the ZED on the base and start it

One camera, no picking sides: the ZED opens as a plain side-by-side webcam and the page finds it by name. Set it on the base, centred left to right, as close to the front edge as it will sit, and prop the front up so it looks at your face rather than at your chest — from down there your head is more than 40 degrees above its own level. Press Start, then check the picture below: your eyes should be near the cross, in both halves. When they are, go to the rig view and press A once while sitting normally — that measures the prop angle, which is the only number here nobody can read off with a ruler.

Where the ZED stands

Measured from the centre of the rig, which is the middle of the acrylic sheet. Height is measured from the sheet, so a camera sitting on the base is negative: minus the height of the acrylic, plus the few centimetres of the ZED's own body that hold the lenses off the board. The baseline is not yours to measure — it is 12 cm on a ZED 2, set at the factory, and that is where the millimetres come from. Toe-in is 0 because the two eyes are parallel. The tilt is the one free number: type it if you measured it, or press A in the rig view and let your own head measure it.

The rig

These already describe the rig as measured: a 16:9 panel above at 45 degrees facing down and toward you, the sheet 6 inches below the panel, black paper 6 inches below the sheet, the whole screen used, the picture sent flipped. Change them only if the rig changes.

Trim the tracker's origin

Go to the rig view, put your head where you normally sit, and look at the cube standing on the base. Move your head left and right. If the cube swims — slides about on the base instead of staying put — nudge the trim until it stops. This trims where the TRACKER thinks its origin is, not the rig. If the hologram is in the wrong place from every angle equally, that is a rig number in step 3, and trimming it away here will only make it swim from somewhere else.

Measure the pair from your own face

The two angles in step 2 are worked out from where you said your head sits — they are a good guess, not a measurement, and a guess that is one degree off moves your tracked head about a centimetre at arm's length. That is not something the trim in step 4 can fix: a trim is a constant shift, and an angle error shifts you differently depending on where you stand, which is exactly what "the hologram swims" means. So measure the angles instead. Your face is the calibration target. Both cameras already find the same 478 points on it, so moving your head round the volume gives hundreds of matched points per second and no printed pattern to hold. Press Start capture, then move about where you normally sit: left and right, nearer and further, higher and lower. The flag on the glass says what is still missing. Then press Solve. The scale cannot come from your face — two cameras watching a face can only see their pose relative to each other, never how big the world is — so it comes from the baseline you measured in step 2. Measure that one with a tape. And the lens fov in step 2 is an input, not a measurement. A focal error does not just move you nearer or further: it bends the toe-in, by roughly a degree and a half for every two degrees of diagonal fov, and at about that much the solved pose is no better than the guess it replaces. Nothing in the solve report can see this — a wrong lens is self-consistent in both cameras, so the residual gets smaller as the pose gets worse. The hold check below is what sees it: it compares how far the pair puts you from itself with how far you said you were, and hands back a fov to type in. Webcam datasheet numbers are nominal and change with the crop the browser picks, so it is worth one round trip.

Then prove it, without taking anything on faith

Put your head at the spot you told the page about in step 2 (), hold still, and this reports how far the measured pair thinks you are from it. A calibration that cannot survive that is not a calibration. The clock only runs while you are actually still, so walking to the spot does not count toward the answer, and the reading freezes once it is a measurement — you can lean over and read it without changing it. Whatever offset is the SAME wherever you stand is what step 4's trim is for. To find out whether yours is, measure it again from a different spot: an offset that moves is an angle or a lens error, and no trim can take that out.
